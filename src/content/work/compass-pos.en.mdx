## Overview

Cygnus Payments is a US payments ISO moving from reselling third-party point of sale to owning the
product. Compass POS is the counter half of that: the terminal staff use all day, the kitchen board
behind it, and the customer-facing display that asks for the tip and the signature.

## Discovery and problem definition

### The problem

Independent restaurants were stitching a counter system together from separate vendors: one for
orders, another for online, another for timekeeping, another for reporting. Nothing agreed, and
staff paid for the disagreement during service.

The design difficulty is not the feature list. It is that a terminal is used by someone with wet
hands, under a heat lamp, with three people waiting, who may have started that morning. Every
screen has to be legible and forgiving under exactly those conditions.

### The goal

A terminal a new hire can run in a single shift, that keeps taking orders when the internet drops,
and that discloses dual pricing correctly without slowing the transaction down.

### My role and responsibilities

Sole product designer for the counter surfaces: information architecture, ordering and payment
flows, the interface, the component library, offline and sync states, and design QA against the
built terminal. I partnered with product and engineering on scope. I did not select hardware or
integrate payment processors.

<Callout kind="constraint">
  Fixed hardware and a fixed screen size. Nothing could be solved by giving the layout more room, so
  every improvement had to come from sequencing, labelling and state.
</Callout>

## Understanding the users

### Research summary

Four people share this terminal. Cashiers and servers who need speed and fewer mistakes. Kitchen
staff reading tickets from three metres away. Owner-operators who live in menus, pricing and
reports. And the Cygnus support team onboarding merchants remotely.

They pull in different directions. Speed for the cashier means fewer taps; accuracy for the kitchen
means more explicit modifiers; control for the owner means more configuration. The architecture had
to serve all three without letting the owner's tools clutter the cashier's screen.

### Journey mapping

Sign in on a PIN, open a ticket, add items, answer required modifiers, take payment with the right
disclosure, fire to the kitchen, close out. Seven steps, and the terminal is judged on the two that
take money.

### Competitive audit

Toast, Square and Clover set the expectation. Matching them feature for feature was not the goal at
this price point, so the differentiators are dual pricing done properly, offline resilience, and a
single design language across surfaces that competitors treat as separate products.

### What testing changed

Two things stuck. Required modifiers moved from a dismissible sheet to one that blocks the ticket,
because staff were sending incomplete items to the kitchen. And the two totals moved onto the ticket
from the start rather than appearing at payment, which removed an argument at the counter.

## Impact, accessibility and reflection

### Accessibility

Targets are sized for a gloved hand, well past 48 by 48 device-independent pixels, and spaced so a
mis-tap lands on empty chrome rather than the wrong item. The kitchen board never relies on colour
alone: every card carries a running timer and a dine-in or to-go word alongside its colour band,
which is what makes it readable to a colourblind cook across a hot line. Required modifiers are
labelled with the word required. Both prices are shown as numerals rather than one price and a
percentage the cashier has to compute.

Design for the worst shift, not the demo. When a product asks for a decision several times in a row,
the design problem is sequencing rather than layout, and the fastest way to reduce mistakes is to
make the incomplete state impossible rather than to warn about it.

The self-service half of the same ecosystem has a different user with a different problem, which is
why it is [its own case study](/work/compass-kiosk).
