## Overview

Texas Flip is the second casino title I designed for Fifty Cats, built on the same table framework
as [Open Omaha](/work/open-omaha) but with a mechanic that doubles the density of the screen. Every
seat holds two hands, and the player may bet on flipping the position of the top card in each.

## The problem

Doubling the hands does not double the difficulty, it squares it. Five seats times two hands is ten
positions, each with an ante, a blind and a potential raise, on a table whose geometry cannot grow.
Two failure modes follow. A player stakes only one of their two hands by accident, or the screen
becomes so dense that nobody stakes anything.

## The goal

Carry the second hand without adding a second learning curve, and make the flip bet, which is the
reason the title exists, understandable at first sight.

## My role

UI and UX for the title, working from the framework I had built for Open Omaha: flow, table layout,
the per-hand state model, visual design and design QA. Shared chip, card and typographic systems
came across from the sister title deliberately.

<Callout kind="constraint">
  A player arriving from Open Omaha should recognise the table immediately. Anything that could stay
  the same had to stay the same, so the novelty budget could be spent entirely on the second hand.
</Callout>

## Understanding the players

### Research summary

The audience is the sister title's audience. That is an advantage and a trap. They already know the
betting rhythm, so the risk is not teaching them the sequence, it is that a familiar-looking table
behaves differently and they discover it after staking.

### The journey

Ante across both hands, optional bonus bets, the flip bet, then a per-hand decision once the
community cards land. The pair of hands travels together until the last step, where it must split.

### Competitive audit

Multi-hand tables usually solve density by shrinking everything. That fails on tablet. The
alternative, which is what shipped, is to treat the pair as one object for as long as possible and
only separate it at the moment the two hands genuinely need different answers.

## Architecture and ideation

### Information architecture

Four passes over the same table, each asking for exactly one class of bet. The instruction line at
the top of the felt changes with the pass, and it is the only place the current question is stated.

### Design system

Blue felt distinguishes the title from its red sibling at a glance while every component underneath
stays shared: the same chip denominations, the same dual total display of bet and balance, the same
control shapes. Hand 1 and Hand 2 are labelled in text under every seat, never distinguished by
position alone.

### What testing changed

The flip bet needed a sentence, not a tooltip. Stating its price as a comparison to a stake already
on the table, rather than as a number, was the change that made people take it.

<Callout kind="decision">
  Treat the pair of hands as one object until the game genuinely needs two. Every step that can be
  answered once for both hands is answered once, which is what keeps ten betting positions from
  reading as ten decisions.
</Callout>

## Accessibility

Ten positions on one table makes target size the binding constraint. Every chip drop zone and
control clears 48 by 48 device-independent pixels on the tablet layout, and adjacent hand positions
are separated by more than a target's width so a mis-tap lands on empty felt rather than the wrong
hand. Hand 1 and Hand 2 are labelled in text. Totals are shown as numbers in two separate fields
rather than as one figure the player has to decompose.

## Impact and reflection

Designing a sister title taught me more about systems than designing the first one. The value was
almost entirely in what I refused to change. A shared framework meant the whole design effort went
into the one thing that was actually new, and a returning player could sit down and read the table
in seconds.
