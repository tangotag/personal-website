## Overview

KOMPETE is a cross-platform multiplayer shooter. My scope was everything around the match: the
lobby a player returns to, the identity pipeline that makes their character theirs, the three
storefronts that sell three different kinds of thing, career progression and the settings nobody
should have to think about.

## The problem

Free-to-play shooters live or die on the loop between matches. That loop is where a player checks
what they earned, decides whether to spend, and starts again. Every studio knows this, which is why
the same loop is usually buried under a storefront that shouts.

Three specific difficulties made this one harder than most.

Cross-platform means the same layout must be driven by a controller stick, a mouse and a
touchscreen. Layouts that assume a cursor fail on a stick; layouts that assume a thumb waste a
monitor.

A shooter's front end is dense by nature. Currency, tickets, rank, friends, invites and match state
all want to be visible at once.

And three storefronts sell three unrelated things. Coins are bought with money, a season pass is
bought with time, an item is bought with coins. Presenting them identically teaches the player
nothing.

## The goal

Get a returning player from launch to a match in one decision, and make every price legible in the
unit the player is actually spending.

## My role

Product and interface design for the front end: competitive teardown, flow mapping, the screen
inventory, the component system, the visual design across all nine screens, and design QA against
the build. I did not design the in-match HUD weapon art or the character models.

<Callout kind="constraint">
  One chrome, three inputs. Anything that only worked with a precise cursor was out, which ruled
  out hover-only affordances, small close targets and dense right-click menus.
</Callout>

## Understanding the players

### Research summary

Two behaviours dominate. The returning player, who opens the game to play and treats everything
else as an obstacle. And the browsing player, who opens it to see what is new, spends time in the
locker and the bazaar, and may not play at all that session. The front end has to serve the first
without hiding the second.

### The journey between matches

Land in the lobby, read what changed, check the squad, maybe adjust the character or spend, then
queue. The only step that is mandatory is the last one, so the play button is the only element that
never moves.

### Competitive audit

The genre leaders put the store on the front door and the match behind a tab. That converts, and it
also trains players to close the game. The alternative taken here keeps the match in the primary
position and gives the stores real estate through the persistent top navigation instead.

## Architecture and ideation

### Information architecture

Seven destinations across one persistent bar: lobby, locker, shop, bazaar, career, leaders and
drops. The bar never changes position or contents, so a player learns it once. Identity, currency
and settings sit at the right end, in the same place on every screen.

### Design system

A dark chrome with a single acid green accent. Green means available or selected, never decoration,
which is what lets a player scan a dense screen at a glance. Every state that green carries is also
carried by a label or a position, so the screens survive a colourblind player and a washed-out
display. Panels are built from one card component with three densities, and the persistent bar is a
single component reused across all nine screens.

## The character pipeline

Three screens of increasing depth, so a player who wants to jump straight into a match never has to
see the second or third. Presets first, then facial morphing, then a points budget across
attributes.

## Progression and settings

Career stats are the reason a player comes back, so they get the same weight and the same layout
grammar as the store rather than being tucked into a profile menu. Settings reuse the game's own
controls instead of inventing an options dialect.

## Accessibility

Cross-platform is an accessibility requirement before it is a technical one. Every target is sized
for a controller stick rather than a mouse, which puts them well past 48 by 48 device-independent
pixels on the touch build. Selection is shown by an outline and a position in the navigation bar,
not by the accent colour alone, so the interface still reads without hue. Quality settings expose
motion blur as its own control, which matters to players who get motion sick, and the settings
screen is reachable from the persistent bar on every screen rather than only from the lobby.

## Impact and reflection

The lesson that transferred straight into product work: a storefront is clearer when it states its
price in the unit the buyer is spending. Coins in money, a pass in time, an item in coins. The same
is true of a subscription page, a usage meter or a checkout, and it is why the three stores here
never became one screen with three tabs.
