## Overview

Open Omaha is a browser poker title for Fifty Cats, played at a five-seat table against the house.
Its difficulty is not the poker. It is that a single hand asks a player for money four separate
times, and each request has to be understood in a couple of seconds by someone who may be new to
the variant.

## The problem

The betting sequence was the product. Ante, blind, a three-card bonus, a four-card bonus, then a
raise decision priced as a multiple of the ante. Presented all at once it reads as a wall of
controls, and a player who does not understand a bet does not place it.

There was a second problem underneath. Omaha is less familiar than Hold'em, and a player who cannot
tell whether they have won is a player who leaves.

## The goal

Raise the share of seated players who complete a full hand and place at least one optional bet, and
make the outcome of every hand legible without a rules page.

## My role

End-to-end UI and UX as the contract senior designer: heuristic audit of the existing table, the
betting sequence, the interface, the icon and chip system, and design QA against the built game. I
did not write the hand-evaluation logic or set the pay tables.

<Callout kind="constraint">
  Fixed table geometry. Five seats and a dealer position cannot move, so every improvement had to
  come from sequencing, labelling and state, not from a new layout.
</Callout>

## Understanding the players

### Research summary

Two groups sit at the same table. Players who know Omaha and want speed, and players who arrived
from a simpler title and are learning as they go. The first group is lost by friction, the second by
confusion. A design that only serves one loses the other.

### The journey, one hand at a time

Buy in, place the ante and blind, decide on the optional bonuses, receive cards, judge the hand,
raise or check, see the result. Seven moments, of which four ask for money.

### Competitive audit

Table titles from the larger studios lean on animation to carry the moment. That was not available
at this budget, so the differentiator had to be clarity: state the price of every bet in the
currency already on the table, and name every hand in words.

## Architecture and ideation

### Information architecture

The table is the whole application. Everything else, settings, coin size, pay table, history and the
cashier, hangs off a single left rail inside a modal, so the player never leaves the felt.

### Design system

A red felt ground with a single warm gold for anything interactive, plus three status colours that
never appear alone: green for a win, red for a loss or a fold, blue for the house. Chip
denominations carry both a colour and a printed number, because colour alone is not a safe way to
convey value. Type is set at sizes that survive a tablet held at arm's length.

### What testing changed

Two things came out of prototype rounds and stayed. The bonus grids moved off the centre of the
table, because players were placing them by accident on the way to the deal button. And the raise
multipliers stopped appearing until the seat was actually being asked, which removed a persistent
question about whether the player had already acted.

## Accessibility

Every control on the felt clears a 48 by 48 device-independent pixel target, which matters on the
tablet layout where a mis-tap costs real money. Status is never colour alone: a win carries a
printed amount, a fold carries the word, and chips carry their denomination as text. Hand names are
words rather than icons, which is also what makes the table usable for a colourblind player reading
red and green suits.

The lesson generalised past casino work. When a product asks for a decision several times in a row,
the design problem is sequencing, not layout. Ask once, price the ask in something the person has
already committed, and say the outcome in words.
