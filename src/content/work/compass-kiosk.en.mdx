## Overview

Compass Kiosk is the self-service half of the Cygnus Payments restaurant ecosystem. A customer walks
up, orders, pays and takes a printed number to the counter, without speaking to anyone. It runs the
same menu, the same modifiers and the same design system as [the counter terminal](/work/compass-pos),
on very different hardware for a very different person.

## Discovery and problem definition

### The problem

A terminal is used by a trained person many times a day. A kiosk is used once, by someone who has
never seen it, with people waiting behind them. Every assumption that makes the counter fast makes
the kiosk hostile.

Three failures follow. People abandon halfway because a screen asked for something they did not
expect. They order the wrong thing because a required choice was easy to miss. Or they freeze at
payment because it was not clear what happens next.

### The goal

Get a first-time customer from attract to a printed order number without help, without a wrong
order, and without ever presenting more than one decision at a time.

### My role and responsibilities

Product design for the self-service surface: the ordering flow, screen inventory, interface,
accessibility pass, and design QA on the physical enclosure. The menu data, modifiers and pricing
come from the same system as the counter, so my work was the journey rather than the catalogue.

<Callout kind="constraint">
  Reach and posture. A kiosk is used standing, sometimes from a wheelchair, so every control has to
  sit in the lower half of the screen, and nothing important may live along the top edge.
</Callout>

## Understanding the users

### Research summary

The kiosk user is defined by three pressures that the counter user does not have. They are
unfamiliar with the interface. They are being watched by the queue. And they have no one to ask.

That combination means the interface has to be its own instruction. Anything that would normally be
explained by a member of staff has to be written on the screen where the decision is made.

### Journey mapping

Attract, choose dine in or take away, browse categories, pick a category, configure the item, review
the order, choose how to pay, pay at the terminal, give a name, give a phone number, take the
receipt. Eleven moments, of which exactly one is mandatory.

### Competitive audit

Most quick-service kiosks front-load: they ask for loyalty sign-in, upsell before the order is
complete, and hide the running total. Each of those adds a decision at the moment a first-timer is
least able to take one. The approach here defers everything that is not the order.

## Architecture and ideation

### Information architecture

A linear journey with one branch, dine in or take away, taken once at the start. Categories are a
grid rather than a menu, and the running order total is visible from the moment there is something
in it.

### Design system

The same tokens, components and type scale as the counter terminal, at kiosk scale. Photography does
the work that labels do on the terminal, because a first-time customer recognises a burger faster
than they read one. A persistent bottom bar carries language and a help number, so the two things a
stuck person needs are never more than a glance away.

### What testing changed

The name and phone steps moved to the end and became skippable. Asking for them before payment read
as a sign-up, and people abandoned. Asked afterwards, framed as how the counter will call you, they
are answered willingly.

## Impact, accessibility and reflection

### Accessibility

Reach drove the layout. Primary controls sit in the lower half of the screen so the kiosk is usable
seated as well as standing, and nothing needed to complete an order lives along the top edge. Targets
clear 48 by 48 device-independent pixels with wide separation, because a mis-tap here costs a wrong
order rather than a corrected one. Language is offered on the first screen and stays on a persistent
bar, along with a help number, so a stuck customer never has to hunt. Required choices are labelled
with the word required, and prices are numerals beside every option rather than a total that changes
without explanation.

### Impact and takeaways

A kiosk is not a terminal with bigger buttons. The counter system optimises for someone who already
knows it; the kiosk has to teach itself to a stranger in the time it takes to order lunch. Sharing
the design system between them was right, but sharing the flow would have been wrong.
